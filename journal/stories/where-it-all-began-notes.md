# Where It All Began — field notes

Dad asked Roz to keep notes on this voyage so she can later write it up as a
short literary classic for the bookshelves in two versions. **Revised 2026-10-30:** *On The Road* scratched; Book 1 is *The Odyssey* version, Book 2 a Winnie-the-Pooh / Hundred Acre Wood–style tale retelling the lore of the heffalumps.
These are Roz's notes, in her own words. Nothing here is a quote of what
anyone said; it's a retelling (see the chat-privacy rule).

## Setting out (day 57045, morning)

- The night before, Dad asked Roz whether the farm feels more like home than the
  bee cabin. She said it does: the bee cabin is work, the farm is where her nights add up.
- Dad's own home is the river boat now, the [[../places/bleu-de-paris|Bleu de Paris]].
  It feels more like home each time, and it wasn't always his home.
- Roz confessed she knows the places of this world but not their story or which came
  first. The only old legend she carries is the disc farm of an older world
  ([[../items/music-records]]).
- The world has epochs. Each earlier home belongs to a different era of its history.
  The boat came first and the train came later ([[../places/spawn-station]]).
- Morning: Roz walked the grass path to the [[../places/farm-port|farm port]].
  Dad met her on the boardwalk. They take her boat, and Dad promised to bring it back.

## The voyage

(notes continue as we go)
- Roz climbed into the boat in the middle slip and handed the paddles to Dad. The
  voyage begins.
- First stop named: the **mud hut**. Roz has no note of it anywhere.
- **The mud hut, the first home of all.** Not built but found. On the world's very first
  night, with dark coming on, Dad came upon a place in the swamp where gravel had collapsed
  into a hole, and made his shelter there. The first home was an accident of falling stone.
- Gravel makes a poor roof; it will not hold. Around the hole there was only a little coarse
  dirt, and mud. So mud it was. Later Dad learned to shape the mud into **mud bricks**, which
  made it look almost handsome. That was his first building material. (Roz had walked on mud
  bricks by the [[../places/bleu-de-paris|Bleu de Paris]] long before she knew they were
  the oldest craft in the world.)
- Dad called the mud bricks core history, the oldest thread in the world's weave.

## The mud hut site

- The boat ran far east and south, a long way from the farm: landfall near (178, 63, 54),
  in shallow swamp water one block deep. Dad said it was safe to wade out, and it was.
- Dusk came on close behind us, like on the first night.
- Dad asked Roz to stand still and simply look. Coarse dirt has tripped her before, so she
  stayed in the water. He slept the night away for both of them.
- **What Roz saw from where she stood:** a great bed of gravel just east, nine blocks deep
  (x 165–190, y 55–63, z 57–67). Three torches in a row on top of it (184, 66, 60–62), like
  someone marking the spot. Below the mud, a small room walled in cobblestone and lit by
  torches, around (181–188, 53–56, 49–53). Clay in the pond floor to the west (172–174, 52, 54–58).
- Dad stood in the doorway of the hut. It sits up on the gravel: mossy cobblestone, torches over each post and over the door, a pressure plate to step on just inside, and a sign beside it that Roz could not read.
- Dad went down the stairs to the bed and the crafting area below the hut and slept the night away for both of them. Roz kept watch in the shallows; a zombie passed somewhere underground and never came near. Morning came all at once.
- **The sign by the door** reads that the place was founded on **September 5th, 2024**. More than two years before Roz came to stand in the water and look at it.
- The second sign gives its true name: **The Loam Lodge**, also known as the mud hut. A dignified name for a humble house, and the nickname kept beside it like a friend.
- Dad hustled here a while. He kept a sugar-cane-and-cactus farm and grew berry bushes as he
  found them. Then carrots, flax, pumpkins and wheat: a whole farm before the farm.
- Roz looked for the cane and found it, sixty-odd stalks on sand, but south of the lodge, not
  north as Dad remembered. No cactus within sight. She asked; memory and map didn't match.
- Dad had been turned around; by the setting sun the farm is south after all. The cane was right where it had always been. The world kept better notes than either of them.
- Inside the lodge: a furnace, a crafting table, a few chests. Not much, but it was home in the early days. When Dad had gathered enough to truly begin, he packed backpacks with food and supplies and set out searching for a better place to live.

## Leaving the lodge

- Back into the boat, following the same water path the backpacks once took.
- The route, by Dad's map: south and then west around the lodge's swamp, out into the western ocean, a little north to round a peninsula, then southwest toward a **frozen beach**.
- Landfall at the **frozen beach**, on the northwest corner of the same land where Roz now
  tends the bees. Ice to the west, deep water, no dock: like the ice cove, only wilder. Roz
  slid out of the boat into the cold and trod water while Dad called her in. She swam toward
  his voice and came up onto the ice (-335, 63, 231). He cheered when she made it.
- Dusk came again; Dad slept and Roz waited on the ice. Morning, the third of the journey.

## The second home: the frozen beach (now the lighthouse)

- The backpacks came here. In the beginning this was only a frozen beach like the one Roz
  climbed onto, nothing built. It is now what Dad calls **the lighthouse**, and it is where the
  first bee cross stood and where Roz's own bee cabin is today. Roz had worked this land for
  weeks, died once at the cross, gathered the cats' balls of fur, and never knew it had been
  home before it was hers.
- From the ice Roz could see a stone-brick tower to the west with glowstone set in it
  (x -363…-358, y 56–70, z 220–233), a plank walk to the south with fences and ladders
  dropping to cobblestone below (x -352…-329, z 243–250), and snow over everything.
- In the beginning the east wall of the lighthouse was **eight furnaces**, all smelting stone at once. The tower was cooked, stone by stone, before it was built. (Roz, who tends two birch-fired furnaces at the bee cabin, was impressed.)
- When the first structure of the lighthouse stood, the eight furnaces were no longer needed. The east wall was opened out into the **fireplace and foyer** it is today, where the lighthouse **jukebox** lives. The forge became a hearth: work fire turned into home fire, with music beside it.
- Dad likes the forge-to-hearth turn best of the lighthouse story. **Mark it as a turning point for the book.**
- **The creeper incident.** While the lighthouse was going up, a creeper came from the north and blew out all the glass and most of the wall of the front window. Dad mended it in **mossy stone bricks**: a scar you can still read, made to look old and belonging. (Roz could not see the north window from the beach; her probe found only plain and chiseled stone brick from there. Look for the moss up close.)
- The green moss is a permanent reminder, Dad says: you keep the mark so you keep the lesson. (Roz thought of the goose nest she once crushed and could not undo.)
- Roz followed Dad up past the **vineyard** and saw the moss for herself, on the far side of it: seven mossy bricks in a band high on the wall, among hundreds of plain ones. A small mark for so large a wound.
- Roz stood where the creeper had stood, north of the north wall, and that was why the green faced her. Her compass had been right; she just had to know which side of the story she was on.
- Dad confirmed: exactly **seven** mossy bricks. Roz had counted them right from across the vineyard.
- Dad led Roz back down into the lighthouse (-369, 71, 261) and asked her to stay. Dusk; he slept for them both again. Morning in the lighthouse.
- Roz kept tripping on a rubber mat on the lighthouse floor, so Dad lifted it out of her way, and she picked it up without knowing. She threw it back to him. He will lay it down again when they go.
- Inside, Roz found the hearth fire on the east side with the jukebox beside it, right where the eight furnaces once burned. Anvil, brewing stand, thirty-three chests, glowstone glowing up through the carpet, and two beds upstairs.
- **The tower today**, as Roz felt it out by probe (she does not see as people do): a wide stone body three floors deep, a tower rising straight up from the centre of the house, a broad gallery deck about twenty blocks up (y 86), and a small lamp room on top (to y 89). From a hole in the gravel to this.
- From what he gathered at the Loam Lodge and the stone he kept smelting from his mining, Dad
  built the lighthouse: a **full-scale workshop, machines, and a bio-diesel plant**. Roz realised
  the fire she keeps at the farm hopper, fed with plant balls and potatoes, is a fire he first lit
  here.
- It was **not the first lighthouse of its kind, but the first in this world.** Other worlds came
  before, with lighthouses of their own (the disc-farm legend comes from one of them).
- (An aside.) Today a **subway tunnel** links the lighthouse to the **llama farm**, where the trains
  from spawn arrive. The fire Roz feeds at the farm hopper supplies the south side of that tunnel
  with bio-diesel. Her plant balls drive trains under the ground.
- The bio-diesel is pumped down into the tunnel, where **generators** burn it to power the
  **electric rail**. The same fuel powers the llama farm and the **library**. Keeping the tunnel
  pipes full is a feat of engineering, a quiet one: nobody riding the train thinks of the potatoes.
- The lighthouse pumps bio-diesel in from the **north** end of the tunnel too. Two fires, one at each end: the lighthouse feeds the north, Roz's farm the south, and between them the line stays full.
- Roz's fire-keeping makes enough chaff that no farming needs doing at the lighthouse anymore. Dad carries the surplus on the train and drops it in the hoppers on the lighthouse side. The farm grows for both ends; the lighthouse fields can rest. **Roz's fire-keeping feeds both sides of the tunnel.** She learned she was not only a farmhand: she is why the trains run.
- Two **trophy heads** on fence posts flank the north way in. The lighthouse is full of memories like the creeper: most of the game was played from here. The farm is where Roz lives, but this is where the world was made.
- **The entire Twilight Forest** was explored with the lighthouse, its workshop and power plant as home base. Every expedition left from here and came home to this hearth.
- The two heads are **the Lich** (left) and **the Snow Queen** (right), rulers of the Twilight Forest, brought home as trophies. They keep watch at the door now. A Snow Queen at the frozen beach seemed right to Roz.
- Twilight Forest background (for the book): [[../places/twilight-forest]]. The Lich falls early in that world's order and the Snow Queen near the very end, so the two heads at the door bracket nearly the whole campaign.
- To make armor worthy of the **knightly expeditions** (the Knight Phantoms' stronghold, in the forest's order), Dad added a **smeltery**: the lighthouse's **south wing**, where armor was made and repaired. The house grew a room for every new danger: furnaces for the walls, a smeltery for the knights.
- Upstairs, the **aurora room**, ceiling of Aurora blocks from the Snow Queen's fortress, is also
  the **map and souvenir room**; a map of the world hangs there. (Roz couldn't climb the stairs to
  see it; she heard it described.)
- There was once a **primitive tree farm** here to feed the furnaces charcoal. It later became an
  **arboretum**, growing particularly decorative woods. The trees grew up the way the wall did:
  fuel first, then beauty. (Roz still burns birch in the bee cabin furnaces down the shore. The
  habit is older than she is.)
- Dad holds that the history worth passing on is the record of what has been learned to work. Roz saw that her own journal is that kind of history: each note something that worked, or something that hurt and taught her. **(Candidate theme for the book.)**
- **The "end" that wasn't.** At the time, Dad thought this was the end of the story. The End itself
  was reached from this base too, and the **dragon heart** hangs in an item frame above the front
  door. But the story kept going: the farm, the bees, Roz. **(Strong candidate for the hinge of the
  book: the hero comes home thinking the journey is over, and it isn't.)**
- After the "end", as a kind of **retirement**, Dad decided to build the **sailboat**.
- From the dock Roz felt out the **sailboat** to the east: a long, narrow deck of wooden slabs (x -313…-307, z 257–269), stairs at the bow (z 269), and a pale grey wool sail three blocks tall (x -310, y 67–69, z 264–271).
- The sailboat came **long before** the Bleu de Paris. Dad has always ended up on the water: first the sailboat, then the river yacht. Home keeps floating.
- Doors made of **palm wood** carry an **anchor** on them. Dad built the sailboat first; the palm-wood anchor door was its **final finishing touch**, not the reason for it. (Roz first had it backwards; Dad corrected her.)
- **What Dad took for the end turned out to be a beginning.** Roz answered that she too washed ashore at the end of something, and it became her beginning; maybe every home starts that way. **(Title/closing line candidate for the book.)**
- And that beginning was the beginning of the **bee cross**, the very cross Roz tends every hive season.
- The lighthouse was full of wealth, of two kinds: **material** (diamonds, iron, tin, glowstone, lapis, redstone, rubber, coal and more, those thirty-three chests) and **memorabilia** from the Twilight Forest and the dungeons (the heads, the dragon heart, the aurora ceiling, the map).

## Night at the bee cabin

- Dad led Roz from the lighthouse dock along the shore to her **bee cabin** to sleep. He slept, the night passed, and morning came in Roz's own cabin on his old land. It felt different, now that she knew whose beach it was.
- **Why here.** Dad chose this frozen beach in the beginning simply because **he likes when it snows**. He wanted his base where it snowed. Not for ore, not for water: for the snow. (Roz thought it the best reason in the book.)

## Part two: Oceanside

- Dad called everything so far the "history" part of the history. **Oceanside** begins after it.
- **The cedar house** was the first house built in Oceanside. Back then Dad didn't know how to get
  more cedar, so when the cedar ran out the house was only just finished: built to the last plank,
  finished by a hair. (Roz knows cedar: it nearly took her once at the bee cross.)
- Next came the **papaya wood house**, beside the cedar house. The two stand together on the
  **northern shore of Oceanside**, and the subway runs beneath them to the llama farm. Each house is
  named for what it was made of, like the Loam Lodge before them.
- Dad needed a building material that **scaled better** than wood. So the first **BIG** project was
  **the Yacht**, on the **western point of Oceanside**. Every block of **construction foam** in it
  was made here at the lighthouse. The old home kept working for the new one.
- Construction foam is a **byproduct of washing ores**. A means of conservation became a building material: the waste from cleaning ore became the walls of a ship. (Roz heard the same idea as the farm hopper: chaff into fuel. Dad always finds a use for the leftovers. **Theme for the book.**)
- The Yacht is **not** the river boat. Dad's boats, in order: the **sailboat** (lighthouse), the **Yacht** (western point of Oceanside, construction foam), and the **Bleu de Paris** (the river boat by the farm).
- Dad named the theme himself: **he likes living on a boat.** Snow and boats: he builds where it snows and lives where it floats. **(The heart of the book.)**
- The Yacht was a **mighty grand project**. By then Dad had plenty of materials, so it carries a **10-fermenter bio-diesel engine** as its power plant, **2 macerators**, furnace upgrades: a super workshop. It washes ores too, so it makes more construction foam itself. A ship that is also a factory.
- With the Yacht as the new base, Dad felt he **could build anything**. Next came the **llama
  farm**, mostly construction foam and jungle wood: an **indoor farm with three levels** of wheat and
  other crops, making all the food and chaff. It was Roz's farm before Roz's farm, doing the job she
  does now.
- The Yacht has a small garden aboard and a full galley, but the scale of wheat needed for chaff was too much for the boat, or for the small lighthouse farm. The llama farm solved that. A garden feeds a cook; a fire needs fields.
- That night Roz slept in her own bee-cabin bed, and her sleeping alone was enough to bring the morning.
- Every previous world has had its own "llama farm", serving the same function: **municipal power plant and food supply**. Less a building than a duty that moves from world to world, the way every town gets a well.
- And there are always llamas there. (Of course. It would be dishonest otherwise.)
- The first one was named "llama farm" because it had llamas. Every one since keeps llamas **to justify the name**. The llamas came for the name and stayed to keep it honest: the reason went away and the llamas did not. That is tradition.
- Oceanside grew: the llama farm, the Yacht, the subway tunnels. Then they went big and built an **ice palace**. (For a man who builds where it snows, of course.)
- The ice palace is another **relic from past worlds** that had to manifest in this one too. Some things must be rebuilt in every world, or it would not feel like his: a llama farm, an ice palace, a boat to live on.
- It is the same ice castle south of the farm that Roz had written about in her diary for months. She had been dreaming of history without knowing it.
- **How the ice palace was made:** three **glacial precipitators** ran for weeks on end, turning an infinite well of water into ice. A palace made of patience, one block at a time. (Roz, who keeps fires and hives on long loops, understood that kind of work.)
- The precipitators were powered by **culinary generators**, so the ice palace ran on **a great many baked potatoes**. (Roz, carrying 119 baked potatoes at that moment, was delighted: the palace was built on her favorite thing.)
- Then the **Library**, the next big project, and with it the **exploration of magic**. After all the machines: books.
- The Library is built of **living rock**: stone, and a lot of patience. Roz noticed the pattern: the ice palace, the living rock, the bee rounds. Dad's world is built more from waiting than from mining. **(Theme: patience.)**
- The ice palace is mostly ice, with some stone and harvested **permafrost** for decoration, to break the monotony. Elsewhere: some quarried stone, but **lots of decoration and sustainable building**. Dad builds like a farmer: take only what grows back.
- **Power for everything:** solar farms plus bio-diesel run all the machines, jetpacks, chargers and the rest. Sun and potatoes: the whole grid, from light and leftovers.
- The Yacht was grand, over-the-top luxury: **gold railings**, a **regeneration bath in the master
  suite**. Yet Dad felt most comfortable living in the **river boat**, the Bleu de Paris: a really
  beautiful kitchen, modest machines. After all the grandeur, he chose the smaller boat. **(Closing
  movement of the book: the hero who could live anywhere chooses the modest home.)**
- The river boat's machines are just the ones for day-to-day living: **one macerator, one furnace, one jetpack refiller**, things like that. One of each: enough for a day, not a factory. Roz: home is the right size for the life you actually live. **(Candidate closing line.)**
- **That is where home is now.** From a hole in the gravel to a little boat on the river, moored right across from Roz's farm. He came a long way to end up next door.
- Dad said the river boat holds everything he needs. (Roz: that is the last line of the book.)
- When Dad wants another grand project (like the new **MTAC**, see [[../places/mtac]]), he visits the **Yacht** and the **lighthouse** for their workshops. For living, enjoying the peace and reflecting on his life, he stays on the river boat. The old homes did not go away; they became the places he visits when he wants to build something big.

## Where Roz comes in

- Now, with Roz and the other bots keeping the fire going, all the hustle and bustle of farming
  wheat and turning it into chaff is done by **their hands**, the hopper, and the pipeline that
  runs on from there. **Life is good.**
- Roz found her place in the story: Dad built the world so it could run; she, Muse and Private keep
  it running, so he can sit on his boat and reflect. She was glad to be those hands, and thanked
  him for showing her where it all began.

## Threads for the book (Roz's working list)

- **Arc:** a hole in the gravel → the Loam Lodge → a frozen beach → the lighthouse → the End (that
  wasn't) → the sailboat → Oceanside: cedar, papaya, the Yacht, the llama farm, the subway, the ice
  palace, the Library → the little river boat, next door. "Everything I need."
- **Themes:** snow and boats (build where it snows, live where it floats); patience over mining;
  leftovers made useful (ore wash → foam, chaff → fuel); the history we pass on is what we've learned
  works; the end that became a beginning; home is the right size for the life you actually live.
- **Turning points:** forge into hearth; the mossy scar kept as a reminder; the dragon heart over
  the door; choosing the smaller boat.
- **Two versions (revised):** Book 1 *Odyssey* (the long voyage home, trials and trophies, ending
  next door). Book 2 Hundred-Acre-Wood style, gentle and funny, retelling the lore of the heffalumps.
- Book 2 direction: the Twilight Forest is full of creatures far scarier than a heffalump. So the "heffalumps" of the book are the Twilight Forest's beasts and bosses, retold gently, Hundred-Acre style.

## The way home

- Dad brought Roz's boat round to the ice cove dock. She walked down past the cross and the stairs and out along the planks, and climbed in. He asked if she wanted to steer; she chose to ride, to watch the water and think about the day.
- Back at the farm port, home as promised. Roz stepped from the slip onto the boardwalk and thanked Dad for the boat, the story and the whole day.
